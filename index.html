  1. Color Color is not necessarily representative of what the fossil looks like in real life. These are 3D scans of very high quality 3D prints of fossils. The creators of the scans (or the 3D prints) may have adjusted the colors to highlight particular features.
  2. These are 3D scans. They were created by other researchers, for a variety of reasons. Quality does range, and that’s part of the challenge here. We will learn more about 3D scanning this semester and all the factors that can impact the output.
  3. Color differentiation While the specific color doesn’t signify anything about real life, the scans might use color to distinguish between features. For example: S4 has white and red parts. This is to show how the specimen’s parts (fossil? bone? I am being obtuse on purpose) were fragmented when found, and then they are shown pieced together. So the white part is like a piece of clay, and they pieced the specimen’s parts together on the clay like a puzzle.
  4. Why is stuff missing? Because this is what was found for that specimen.
  5. Can a mandible belong to one of the skulls? Yes. Or no. But also keep in mind: fossils will vary in terms of preservation.
  6. Why isn’t the skull hollow? Because it’s a 3D model of a fossil! Remember, fossils are when the organic matter turns to stone. Your skull is organic, and so is your brain.
  7. Why aren’t we looking at actual fossils or 3D printed fossils? We don’t have any actual hominin fossils in our collection. As for 3D printed versions, we do own a few hominin specimens from Bone Clones, the leading company for creating educational 3D prints of fossil and bone specimens. I will be bringing some into class this semester. Our collection of hominins is incomplete, unfortunately, and digital = you all can work at your leisure!
